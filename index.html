The Image Compression Function

Here's how I set this up: for an image stored as an n × p matrix X, I form the cross product V = X′X, eigendecompose it as V = UΛU′, and get the principal component scores Z = XU. The rank-k approximation just keeps the first k columns of Z and U: X(k) = ZkUk′. For the error I just take the Frobenius norm of whatever's left over, X − X(k). 

compress_image <- function(x, k) {
  x <- as.matrix(x)
  p <- ncol(x)

  if (k < 1 || k > p) {
    stop("k must be between 1 and ncol(x)")
  }

  V <- t(x) %*% x            # p x p cross product
  eig <- eigen(V, symmetric = TRUE)
  U <- eig$vectors           # p x p eigenvectors
  Z <- x %*% U                # n x p PC scores

  Uk <- U[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]
  Zk <- Z[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]

  approx <- Zk %*% t(Uk)     # rank-k approximation of x
  error <- norm(x - approx, type = "F")  # Frobenius norm of the residual

  list(approx = approx, error = error, U = U, Z = Z)
}

Image 1 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

Looking at u₁, it's flat at zero across almost all 100 columns except for one sharp spike around column 78. That lines up with a real feature in the original image itself: a thin bright vertical stripe near the right side (you can see it in the "Original" panel above). So the first component isn't summarizing the whole image, it's mostly just isolating that one unusually bright column, which is why the rank-1 through rank-10 reconstructions look like flat gray everywhere except that one stripe. z₁ down the rows is spiky rather than smooth, which tracks how that stripe's brightness bounces around row by row. The rest of the image (everything besides that stripe) is a lot noisier and needs many more components: it takes all the way to k = 63 to hit 95% of the variance and k = 91 for 99%, which is basically the whole image.

If I had to pick one k for this image, I'd go with somewhere around k ≈ 60, right where it crosses that 95% mark. Anything in the k = 10–20 range still reads as pure noise to me, so I wouldn't make any good conlusions until you're most of the way to full rank.

Image 2 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

This one's the complete opposite of the last one. A single component already explains basically 100% of the variance, and the rank-1 panel in the grid above is pretty much indistinguishable from the original. Looking at the plots, z₁ is doing most of the work: it swings wildly from row to row (from about -3000 up to 4000), and that's what creates the horizontal banding you see in the grid. u₁ itself is just a smooth, steadily declining line across the 100 columns, no zigzagging. So each row's brightness is basically set by that row's z₁ value, which is why the bands run flat left to right instead of looking tilted or jagged. There's really only one mode doing all the work here, so the rank-1 outer product z₁u₁′ reconstructs almost the whole image by itself.

Easiest call of the four. k = 1 is genuinely enough here. I'd bump it to 2 just so I'm not putting everything on a single number, but that's more safe than necessity.

Image 3 (376 × 345)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

You can actually tell this is a dog by about k = 2 or 3, and by k = 10 it's pretty sharp, and that's already 95.2% of the total variance. Looking at u₁, there's a big dip across the middle two-thirds of the 345 columns, which lines up with the dark body of the dog sitting between lighter background on either side. z₁ peaks over roughly rows 60–160 of the 376 rows, which is right where the dog sits vertically in the frame. So the rank-1 piece alone (z₁u₁′) is already sketching out the animal's silhouette before any of the other components get added.

k = 10 feels like the sweet spot to me. You're at 95.2% of the variance and the picture already reads clearly as a dog. If I wanted the edges a bit crisper I'd push it up toward k ≈ 25–33 (99%), but I don't think it's necessary.

Image 4 (213 × 119)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

This one is a mottled, textured image with a distinct bright rectangular patch a bit right of center, plus a couple of smaller bright patches scattered around it. It's recognizable by about k = 5, and by k = 10 the whole texture is legible, at 93.9% of the total variance (95% kicks in right at k = 11). u₁ has its biggest dip around column 85 of the 119 columns, and z₁ has its biggest dip around row 145 of the 213 rows. Since both are at their most negative in those spots, their product is large and positive right there, which is exactly where that bright rectangular patch sits in the image. That's also why a hint of the patch already shows up in the rank-1 approximation, before any of the other components are added.

I'd call k = 10–12 good enough here. The overall texture and that bright patch are both clearly visible by then, and I don't think it gets meaningfully better until you're way further out.
