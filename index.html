The Image Compression Function

Given an image stored as an n × p matrix X, the function below forms the cross product V = X′X, eigendecomposes it as V = UΛU′, and computes the principal component scores Z = XU. The rank-k approximation keeps only the first k columns of Z and U: X(k) = ZkUk′. The approximation error is the Frobenius norm of the residual X − X(k). 

compress_image <- function(x, k) {
  x <- as.matrix(x)
  p <- ncol(x)

  if (k < 1 || k > p) {
    stop("k must be between 1 and ncol(x)")
  }

  V <- t(x) %*% x            # p x p cross product
  eig <- eigen(V, symmetric = TRUE)
  U <- eig$vectors           # p x p eigenvectors
  Z <- x %*% U                # n x p PC scores

  Uk <- U[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]
  Zk <- Z[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]

  approx <- Zk %*% t(Uk)     # rank-k approximation of x
  error <- norm(x - approx, type = "F")  # Frobenius norm of the residual

  list(approx = approx, error = error, U = U, Z = Z)
}

Image 1 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

At k = 10 the reconstruction already captures 83.0% of the total variance, yet it still looks like a field of fine-grained texture — there is no single dominant large-scale shape for a handful of components to reconstruct. The first eigenvector u₁ oscillates rapidly across all 100 columns rather than tracing out one smooth, dominant pattern, which is the signature of an image whose energy is spread thinly across many components instead of concentrated in the first few. The first PC score z₁ behaves the same way down the rows: it doesn't follow a smooth trend, so no single row-wise "intensity profile" explains most of the picture. Reaching 95% of the variance takes k = 63, and 99% takes k = 91 — almost the full rank of 100.

My opinion: Image 1 is the least compressible of the four. I would call the approximation "sufficiently good" only around k ≈ 60–65, where it crosses the 95% variance-explained mark; anything under k = 10–20 still looks indistinguishable from noise.

Image 2 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

Image 2 is the opposite extreme: a single component (k = 1) already explains essentially 100% of the variance, and the rank-1 panel in the grid above is visually indistinguishable from the original. The image is dominated by one repeating horizontal-banding pattern, so the first eigenvector u₁ is a single smooth profile across the 100 columns that captures that banding directly, and the first PC score z₁ simply traces how the intensity of those bands rises and falls down the 1000 rows. Because almost all of the structure lives in this one mode, the rank-1 outer product z₁u₁′ alone reconstructs nearly the entire image.

My opinion: Image 2 is extremely compressible. k = 1 is already sufficient; I'd use k = 2 only for a small margin of safety.

Image 3 (376 × 345)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

This image (a rabbit against a light background) is recognizable by k = 2–3 and quite sharp by k = 10, which already captures 95.2% of the total variance. The first eigenvector u₁ has a pronounced dip across the middle third of the 345 columns — that's the dark rabbit body standing out against the lighter blanket/background on either side. The first PC score z₁ rises to a broad peak over roughly rows 60–160 of the 376 rows, which is exactly the vertical band where the rabbit's body sits in the frame. Together, the outer product z₁u₁′ is largest exactly where the dark rabbit region overlaps that row band, which is why the rank-1 approximation already outlines the animal's silhouette even before any higher components are added.

My opinion: k = 10 is already a very good trade-off (95.2% of variance, clearly recognizable). If I wanted crisper edges I'd go up to about k ≈ 25–33 (99% of variance), but 10 is sufficient for most purposes.

Image 4 (213 × 119)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

Image 4 (an outdoor scene with a bright sign) is recognizable by about k = 5 and clearly legible — sign included — by k = 10, which captures 93.9% of the total variance (95% is reached at k = 11). The first eigenvector u₁ picks out the contrast between the bright sign region and the darker surroundings across the 119 columns, and the first PC score z₁ tracks how strongly that bright/dark contrast shows up as you scan down the 213 rows, peaking near the row band where the sign actually sits.

My opinion: k = 10–12 is sufficient here — the scene layout and the sign text region are both clearly legible by that point.
