The Image Compression Function

Here's how I set this up: for an image stored as an n × p matrix X, I form the cross product V = X′X, eigendecompose it as V = UΛU′, and get the principal component scores Z = XU. The rank-k approximation just keeps the first k columns of Z and U: X(k) = ZkUk′. For the error I just take the Frobenius norm of whatever's left over, X − X(k). 

compress_image <- function(x, k) {
  x <- as.matrix(x)
  p <- ncol(x)

  if (k < 1 || k > p) {
    stop("k must be between 1 and ncol(x)")
  }

  V <- t(x) %*% x            # p x p cross product
  eig <- eigen(V, symmetric = TRUE)
  U <- eig$vectors           # p x p eigenvectors
  Z <- x %*% U                # n x p PC scores

  Uk <- U[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]
  Zk <- Z[, 1:k, drop = FALSE]

  approx <- Zk %*% t(Uk)     # rank-k approximation of x
  error <- norm(x - approx, type = "F")  # Frobenius norm of the residual

  list(approx = approx, error = error, U = U, Z = Z)
}

Image 1 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

I was suprised by this one. At k = 10 we're already capturing 83.0% of the variance, but the reconstruction still just looks like noise. There's no single big shape for a handful of components to latch onto. You can see it in the eigenvector plot too: u₁ is jumping up and down across all 100 columns instead of settling into one smooth curve, which tells me whatever structure is here isn't concentrated in a few directions, it's just gathered across a lot of them. Same story for z₁ down the rows, no real trend, just variance. It takes all the way to k = 63 to hit 95% of the variance and k = 91 for 99%, which is basically the whole image.

If I had to pick one k for this image, I'd go with somewhere around k ≈ 60, right where it crosses that 95% mark. Anything in the k = 10–20 range still reads as pure noise to me, so I wouldn't make any good conlusions until you're most of the way to full rank.

Image 2 (1000 × 100)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

This one's the complete opposite as the last one. A single component already explains basically 100% of the variance, and the rank-1 panel in the grid above is pretty much indistinguishable from the original. It makes sense once you look at the eigenvector: u₁ is one smooth profile across the 100 columns that's basically capturing the horizontal banding pattern on its own, and z₁ just tracks how strong that banding is as you move down the 1000 rows. There's really only one mode doing all the work here, so the rank 1 outer product z₁u₁′ reconstructs almost the whole image by itself.

Easiest call of the four. k = 1 is genuinely enough here. I'd bump it to 2 just so I'm not putting everything on a single number, but that's more safe than necessity.

Image 3 (376 × 345)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

You can actually tell this is a dog by about k = 2 or 3, and by k = 10 it's pretty sharp, and that's already 95.2% of the total variance. Looking at u₁, there's a big dip across the middle third of the 345 columns, which lines up with the dark body of the dog sitting between lighter background on either side. z₁ peaks over roughly rows 60–160 of the 376 rows, which is right where the dog sits vertically in the frame. So the rank-1 piece alone (z₁u₁′) is already sketching out the animal's silhouette before any of the other components get added.

k = 10 feels like the sweet spot to me. You're at 95.2% of the variance and the picture already reads clearly as a dog. If I wanted the edges a bit crisper I'd push it up toward k ≈ 25–33 (99%), but I don't think it's necessary.

Image 4 (213 × 119)

Original and rank 1–10 approximations

Interpretation

This scene (outdoor shot with a bright sign) is readable by about k = 5, and by k = 10 the whole thing (sign included) is legible, at 93.9% of the total variance (95% kicks in right at k = 11). u₁ is basically picking out the contrast between the bright sign and the darker surroundings across the 119 columns, and z₁ tracks how strong that contrast is as you move down the 213 rows, peaking right around where the sign sits.

I'd call k = 10–12 good enough here. The layout and the sign are both clearly readable by then, and I don't think it gets meaningfully better until you're way further out.
